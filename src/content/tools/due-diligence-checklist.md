## How it works

Pick an organization, or start a new one, and work through the questions in any order. Each question says why it matters and, where a public record can answer it, how to check. Mark each one **yes**, **no**, **unknown** or **not applicable**, and add a note if you like. The checklist keeps a separate copy for every organization you check, saved only in this browser.

The summary counts answers by status. Anything marked unknown is listed as a question to ask the organization, and the whole checklist downloads as a Markdown report you can share, edit or keep.

If you came from the [Charity Lookup](/tools/charity-lookup/), the organization’s name is already filled in. Links to ProPublica and WHOFUNDS then open with that name searched, which sends it to those sites when you click.

## Six groups of questions

**Legitimacy** asks whether the organization is what it says it is: recognized by the IRS, still filing, registered where your state requires it, and governed by people who can check it. **Money** asks what the spending buys and who else pays, and deliberately doesn’t ask for an overhead ratio, because a low one tells you little about results. That is [the Overhead Myth](/diagnosis/overhead-myth/). **Results** asks for outcomes, not just outputs, which is [the Measurement Gap](/diagnosis/measurement-gap/). **Learning** asks whether the organization publishes what didn’t work and changes because of it, the cure for [the Silence About Failure](/diagnosis/silence-about-failure/).

**The system lens** asks what no tax form shows: where on the [depth scale](/method/#depth) the work acts, what would happen without it, whether it coordinates with others and whether the people it serves have a say. That last one is [the Missing Voice](/diagnosis/missing-voice/). **Red flags** are warning signs the Federal Trade Commission describes. In that group, yes means you saw the sign.

## What it isn’t

It isn’t a score, and it never adds answers into a grade. A “no” is information, not a verdict: a young organization may not have an audit yet, and a tiny one may have no reason to publish a failure report. Unknowns are questions, never failures. The registries it points to are U.S. sources run by other organizations, and Charitology doesn’t vouch for what they show. A clean checklist doesn’t prove an organization is effective, and seeing a red flag doesn’t prove a scam. It means stop and check.
