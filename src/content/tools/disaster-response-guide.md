## How it works

The guide opens with six points drawn from disaster responders, regulators and researchers, each with its sources. Then the planner splits one amount across three phases: **relief now**, **recovery later** and **preparedness** for the next disaster. Move any slider and the other two rebalance, so the total stays at 100%. The reminder makes a calendar file for a date you choose, six months out unless you change it, so the part of your gift meant for recovery has a moment to happen. The file is built in your browser and nothing is sent anywhere.

## Why the planner splits by phase

The three phases sit at different depths on the [depth scale](/method/#depth): relief meets the need today, recovery repairs what was lost, and preparedness prevents the next disaster from hurting as much. All three are needed.

Disaster giving follows attention. Thomas Eisensee and David Strömberg found that U.S. government disaster relief depended partly on whether other news crowded a disaster out of the headlines. That is [the Story Bias](/diagnosis/story-bias/). Gifts surge in the first days, but recovery can take years, which is [the Short Horizon](/diagnosis/short-horizon/). In 2023, according to the Center for Disaster Philanthropy and Candid, reconstruction and recovery received 12.7% of tracked disaster giving and resilience, risk reduction and mitigation received 6%.

The default split is 50% relief, 30% recovery and 20% preparedness. It is an editorial starting point, not a finding: it puts a larger share toward recovery than disaster giving does today and keeps a real share for preparedness, so no phase is left out. Change it to fit what you know. If responders say their needs are met, give flexibly rather than to one disaster, which is [the Restriction Reflex](/diagnosis/restriction-reflex/) in reverse.

## What it isn’t

It isn’t a recommendation of any organization, and it doesn’t know the details of a particular disaster. Responders on the ground do, so follow their requests when they differ from the defaults. The guidance comes mostly from U.S. sources, and the scam checks point to U.S. registries. Calendar apps treat reminder files differently, so check that the event and its alert appear where you expect.
