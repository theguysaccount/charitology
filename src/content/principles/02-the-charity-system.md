## A system produces what it is built to produce

Chapter 1 made a claim: charity is a system, and like any system it produces the results it is built to produce. This chapter draws the system. It is the map the rest of the book works from, and it is the frame for [the Diagnosis](/diagnosis/), the fifteen conditions that describe how the system goes wrong.

The map has five stages, a loop that should connect the last stage to the first, and a field around the whole thing. A short version is in the Encyclopedia under [charity as a system](/record/encyclopedia/charity-as-a-system/).

## Five stages

**1. Givers.** The people and institutions who decide to give. In the United States, Giving USA estimates that $617.2 billion was given in 2025. Individuals gave $394.2 billion of it, about 64 percent. Foundations gave about $117 billion, bequests about 10 percent of the total and corporations about 7 percent. Givers bring more than money: they bring values, habits, loyalties and limited time to decide.

**2. Signals.** Givers decide on what they can see. That is a short list: a moving story or a face, a name they trust, an overhead ratio printed on a tax form, a ranking, the prestige of a gala or a building, the tax break. Signals are the system’s information layer, and three conditions of the Diagnosis act here.

**3. Channels.** Money then moves through intermediaries: operating charities, foundations, donor-advised funds, giving platforms and federated campaigns. The IRS master file of tax-exempt organizations listed nearly two million records in October 2026. Donor-advised funds alone held about $326 billion in 2024. Channels decide how fast money moves, how many strings come with it and how many separate organizations share the work.

**4. The work.** What the money pays for. Charitology describes it by where it acts on the [depth scale](/record/encyclopedia/source-depth/), from relief through repair, prevention and structure to source. Chapter 3 is about that scale.

**5. Outcomes.** What actually changes: lives saved or improved, institutions changed, futures protected. Outcomes are not the same as the activity that produced them, and they are much harder to see. See [outputs and outcomes](/record/encyclopedia/outputs-and-outcomes/).

## The loop that isn’t there

In most of economic life, information flows back. When you buy a phone, the price, the reviews and your own experience tell you and everyone else whether it was worth it. A product that fails loses customers.

Charity breaks that loop at its root. The person who pays is usually not the person served. In 1980 the legal scholar Henry Hansmann described the general problem as *contract failure*: the people paying for a service cannot easily judge its quality. Donors funding services for other people cannot see what their money did. Fay Twersky, Phil Buchanan and Valerie Threlfall made the comparison with business in 2013: a company that ignores its customers watches its sales fall, but the people a charity serves often have few alternatives and are not the ones paying, so a weak program can carry on.

The result is that the signals reaching givers are not results. They are whatever is easy to show. That is why Charitology treats [the Missing Feedback Loop](/diagnosis/missing-feedback-loop/) as the root condition. Almost every other condition grows in the space it leaves open.

## The field around it

Around the five stages sits the field: the people and knowledge that could steer the system as a whole. There are scholars of philanthropy, evaluators, advisors and researchers in every cause. What is missing is a shared craft whose subject is the system itself, with methods, training and standards. That is [the Missing Profession](/diagnosis/missing-profession/), and it is the reason this book exists.

## Problems travel

The point of drawing a system is that problems travel between stages. Take the overhead ratio. It starts as a signal: a number donors can find when they cannot find results. It moves into the channels as caps on indirect costs. Ann Goggins Gregory and Don Howard found that foundation allowances for indirect costs averaged 10 to 15 percent of a grant, while the true overhead of four youth-serving nonprofits they studied ran from 17 to 35 percent. It then reaches the work and the outcomes as underpaid staff, old systems and skipped evaluation, which deepens the [Measurement Gap](/diagnosis/measurement-gap/). Gregory and Howard called the loop the nonprofit starvation cycle.

A system view changes the questions a giver asks. “Is this a good charity?” becomes “What does the system reward, and is this gift pushing against it or with it?”

## Where to push

The systems thinker Donella Meadows argued that most attention goes to the numbers in a system, such as budgets, rates and targets, although they offer little leverage. Higher leverage lies in the structure of information flows, the rules, the goals and the mindset the system grows from. Charitology’s bet follows from that. Closing the feedback loop is an information-flow change. Changing what the system rewards is a rule change. Naming the field is a change of mindset.

That bet is a hypothesis. How strongly each link in the map holds has not been measured, and several of the [Open Problems](/open-problems/) exist to test it, starting with [Closing the Feedback Loop](/open-problems/closing-the-feedback-loop/) and [the Depth Census](/open-problems/the-depth-census/). To see your own place on the map, start with the [Giving Dashboard](/tools/giving-dashboard/) or look up an organization in the [Charity Lookup](/tools/charity-lookup/).
