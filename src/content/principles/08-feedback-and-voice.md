## Two people, not one

In a market, the buyer and the user are usually the same person. If a product is bad, the person who paid for it knows, and the next time they pay for something else. Prices and reviews carry what each buyer learns to everyone else. Those signals can help, though markets also have information failures.

In donor-funded services, the payer and the person served can be different people. A donor in one city pays for a clinic in another country. The patients know whether the clinic helped. The donor may need other evidence. In 1980 Henry Hansmann described the general problem as *contract failure*: the people paying for a service cannot easily judge its quality. Charitology calls the result [the Missing Feedback Loop](/diagnosis/missing-feedback-loop/) and treats it as the root of the Diagnosis.

Two gaps can arise. Results may not reach givers, and the knowledge of the people served may not influence funding decisions. That second gap is [the Missing Voice](/diagnosis/missing-voice/).

## What a loop would look like

A complete feedback loop has three parts. The people served say how the work is going. Someone listens and changes the work. And the money responds: what works attracts more, and what doesn’t attracts less. How often charity contains all three, and when that improves outcomes, are questions for evidence rather than assumptions.

## Listening

In 2013 Fay Twersky, Phil Buchanan and Valerie Threlfall argued that funders and nonprofits consult experts and crowds but rarely ask the people they serve in any systematic way. They pointed to fields that do listen. In health care, research had found that patients’ perceptions of their care, especially nursing care, predicted clinical quality and lower death rates among heart attack patients. In education, the YouthTruth initiative had surveyed about 142,000 students, and 98 percent of the participating school leaders said they used or planned to use the results. The views of the people served, they argued, can be a leading indicator of trouble.

They were candid about the costs. Good feedback is expensive to collect, people with low incomes or low literacy are hard to survey well, and people may fear that honest criticism will cost them help. Feedback works alongside other evidence, not in place of it. See [beneficiary feedback](/record/encyclopedia/beneficiary-feedback/).

A small infrastructure has grown up to make listening normal. Feedback Labs supports funders and nonprofits to listen to people and act on what they hear. Fund for Shared Insight, a national funder collaborative, worked on the same goal for 12 years before concluding in 2026, and launched Listen4Good to give nonprofits tools and coaching for collecting feedback.

## Sharing decisions

Listening still leaves the decision with the funder. Some funders go further and share it. In [participatory grantmaking](/record/encyclopedia/participatory-grantmaking/), described in Cynthia Gibson’s 2018 guide for GrantCraft, funders cede decision-making power about funding to the communities they aim to serve. [Trust-based philanthropy](/record/lexicon/#trust-based-philanthropy) works on the relationship between funders and grantees: its six practices include giving multi-year unrestricted funding and soliciting and acting on feedback. When the COVID-19 pandemic began in March 2020, a pledge led by the Ford Foundation asked funders to listen to the communities least heard, alongside loosening restrictions on their grants.

## Letting people choose

The most direct way to put the people served in charge is to give them the money. In [cash transfers](/record/interventions/cash-transfers/), recipients decide what to buy, so every purchase is a decision about what they need most. It is not the right answer to every problem, since some goods, such as a vaccination campaign or a new law, cannot be bought one household at a time. But it raises a useful question for any program that spends money on people’s behalf: would they be better off with the money itself?

## Feedback to givers

The loop also has a giver’s end. What a donor sees after giving shapes the next gift. That information might include a thank-you letter, accounts, feedback or evaluation results. A system with a working loop would show givers what changed, honestly, including when nothing did. The [Giving Dashboard](/tools/giving-dashboard/) and [Charitology Wrapped](/tools/charitology-wrapped/) are meant to give the giver’s end of the loop something better to show.

## What would close it

Each practice in this chapter repairs part of the loop. This chapter does not establish which of these practices links the voice of the people served to funding at scale, or how reliably. What mechanism could? That is [Open Problem 5, Closing the Feedback Loop](/open-problems/closing-the-feedback-loop/). It targets the root condition of the Diagnosis: Charitology hypothesizes that a better loop could also improve other conditions.

A related question needs context-specific comparison: do community-led programs outperform top-down ones on the same outcome, and by how much? That is [Open Problem 14, the Agency Premium](/open-problems/the-agency-premium/). Differences in outcomes would inform program design. They would not by themselves settle whose rights or decision authority should count.
