## What it is

Public budgets for aid and social programs face the same questions as private giving: what works, where on the [depth scale](/method/#depth) the money acts and how far ahead it looks. The Policy Program would apply Charitology’s research to those budgets and publish what it finds for anyone to use.

## How it works

Each analysis would take one public program or budget line and ask four questions. What does the evidence say about the interventions it pays for, drawing on [Evidence Reviews](/programs/evidence-reviews/)? How is the money split between relief, repair, prevention, structure and source? What are its costs and benefits over ten or twenty years, not just one budget cycle? And who was consulted on its design, including the people it serves?

The program would build on government work that already exists. The UK’s What Works Network, made up of independent What Works Centres, uses evidence to improve how public services are designed and delivered. In the United States, the Foundations for Evidence-Based Policymaking Act, which became law in January 2019, requires federal agencies to plan how they will build evidence for policy and to name an evaluation officer.

The program would publish analysis. It wouldn’t lobby for bills, endorse candidates or take sides between parties.

## Why it matters for the system

Charitology’s hypothesis is that public budgets feel the same pulls as private giving. The [Symptom Trap](/diagnosis/symptom-trap/) favors relief that is visible today, and the [Short Horizon](/diagnosis/short-horizon/) favors results that show up within a budget cycle, while the payoff from prevention can take years. Showing a program’s depth mix and long-run costs would give budget makers the view Charitology asks private givers to take, and would test whether the hypothesis holds for public money.

## Risks and limits

Private analysis of public money raises its own question about influence without accountability. That is [Open Problem 21, the Government Question](/open-problems/the-government-question/). The program would publish its methods and data, invite the agencies it studies to respond and stay out of partisan politics.

## What success looks like

Within five years, the program has published analyses of several public programs and at least one has been cited in a public budget debate. Agencies have used its depth-and-horizon method in their own evaluations.
