## What it is

The Principles of Charitology Course is a free introduction to the field for anyone who gives, works at a nonprofit or advises people who do. It is built on [Principles of Charitology](/record/principles/), the open textbook written one chapter at a time, and it needs no account and no fee.

## How it works

Each chapter of the textbook is the core of a unit with three parts:

- **A reading**, which is the chapter itself, with key terms linked to the [Lexicon](/record/lexicon/).
- **An exercise** that puts the idea to work with a real tool, such as placing a gift on the [depth scale](/method/#depth), comparing frameworks in the [Values Compass](/tools/values-compass/) or estimating what would have happened without a gift.
- **A problem set** with worked answers, so learners can check themselves.

The course would end with a capstone: write up one giving decision, real or hypothetical, using the [Giver’s Checklist](/method/#checklist), and publish it if you choose. Teachers are welcome to use any unit in their own classes.

## Why it matters for the system

The [Missing Profession](/diagnosis/missing-profession/) can’t be fixed by a few experts. A craft spreads when anyone can learn it. A free course cuts the cost of learning charitology down to the time it takes, which matters most for people outside wealthy institutions and countries.

The course also serves the rest of the field. It would be the study path for the [Certified Charitologist](/programs/certified-charitologist/) credential, and it is a ready-made curriculum for [Open Problem 17, the Profession Question](/open-problems/the-profession-question/), which asks whether training changes where money goes.

## Risks and limits

A course can teach a method without showing that the method works. That is why each unit is meant to point learners to the open problems it touches, and why Charitology labels its own founding claims as hypotheses.

## What success looks like

Within three years, every published chapter has a full unit and at least ten teachers use the course in their own classes. A randomized study of a short version has tested whether it changes how people give.
