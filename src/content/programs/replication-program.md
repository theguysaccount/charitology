## What it is

A finding that has been tested once is a lead, not a fact. In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration repeated 100 published psychology studies. Ninety-seven percent of the originals had reported statistically significant results. Only 36 percent of the repeats did. Nobody knows the equivalent rate for charity impact research. That is [Open Problem 10, the Replication Rate](/open-problems/the-replication-rate/), and the Replication Program exists to answer it.

## How it works

A replication can mean re-analyzing the original data or running the study again somewhere new. The program would do both. It would choose studies by fixed rules, such as how often they are cited and how much money relies on them, and publish the list and the analysis plan before any results come in. Every result would be published, including the ones that hold up.

Charity research has done this before, in public. In 2015 a team re-analyzed an influential 1998–99 school deworming study from western Kenya, using the original data and methods. It was possible because the original researchers shared their data and analysis files. Most results were reproduced. Some were not, including an effect on attendance at nearby schools, for which little evidence remained once coding errors were corrected. The original researchers replied in the same issue of the journal. The program aims for the same openness every time: shared data, published plans and replies printed next to results.

## Why it matters for the system

The [Silence About Failure](/diagnosis/silence-about-failure/) applies to research too. A standing program makes re-testing routine, so a finding that doesn’t hold up is published as ordinary news rather than buried or treated as a scandal. It also treats the [Measurement Gap](/diagnosis/measurement-gap/) from the other side. Measurement helps only if the measurements can be trusted, and replication is how a field learns which ones can.

## Risks and limits

A failed replication doesn’t mean the first team did anything wrong. Places change, programs change and chance plays a part. The program would report results without blame and invite the original authors to respond alongside every result.

## What success looks like

Within five years, the program has published replications of at least 20 influential findings and an estimate of the replication rate with confidence bounds, and donors can see a replication status next to the studies they rely on.
