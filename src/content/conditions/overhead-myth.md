## What it looks like

The question donors ask most is how much of a gift goes “to the cause.” A low overhead ratio became a badge of virtue and a high one a warning sign. In 2013 the leaders of GuideStar, Charity Navigator and the BBB Wise Giving Alliance, the groups that had helped make those ratios popular, published an open letter telling donors that overhead is a poor measure of a charity’s performance and asking them to look at transparency, governance, leadership and results instead.

## Why it happens

Overhead is printed on a tax form. Impact isn’t printed anywhere. So the number that is easy to find stands in for the number that matters. Charities respond by underpaying staff, running on old technology and skipping evaluation, then understating what those things cost so their ratios stay low. Ann Goggins Gregory and Don Howard called this loop “the nonprofit starvation cycle.”

A ratio of costs says nothing about results. A charity can spend almost nothing on overhead and achieve nothing. Another can invest heavily in good systems and change thousands of lives.

## No one is to blame

Asking where your money goes is a responsible instinct. The measure people reach for just can’t answer the question they are asking.

## What we don’t know yet

What simple public measure could replace the overhead ratio as the default way donors judge a charity, without being gamed? That is [Open Problem 6, the Overhead Replacement](/open-problems/the-overhead-replacement/). The data to start from already exists: [Charity Impact Media](https://theguysaccount.github.io/charity-impact-media/) publishes sourced Form 990 spending for about 25,000 U.S. charities, with no ratings attached.
