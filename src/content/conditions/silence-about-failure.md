## What it looks like

Annual reports are full of success stories. Programs that didn’t work tend to vanish quietly. When another group tries the same idea a few years later, it starts from zero.

## Why it happens

In medicine, a failed drug trial gets published and the next team learns from it. In charity, a published failure can cost the next grant. So the safe move is silence, and the field loses the most valuable data it produces: what doesn’t work.

A few organizations have pushed the other way. Engineers Without Borders Canada published failure reports and started Admitting Failure, a site where people in development share what went wrong. Charitology’s [Failure Archive](/record/failure-archive/) exists for the same reason.

## No one is to blame

Nobody wants to fail in public, least of all when people’s jobs and the people they serve depend on the next grant. The incentive is the problem, not the people facing it.

## What we don’t know yet

What incentives would get charities to publish failures, and what happens to their funding when they do? That is [Open Problem 9, the Failure Commons](/open-problems/the-failure-commons/).
