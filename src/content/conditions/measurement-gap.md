## What it looks like

A hypothetical charity reports 10,000 meals served, 500 students tutored and 2,000 nets handed out. Those are *outputs*. Whether anyone is healthier, better educated or less hungry a year later is a different and harder question. Those are *outcomes*.

## Why it happens

Counting activity can be simpler than estimating its effects. Establishing causal impact may require comparison groups, follow-up surveys or specialist help. Routine outcome monitoring does not always need a trial. The 2019 Nobel Prize in economics went to Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo and Michael Kremer for an experimental approach to measuring what anti-poverty programs really change. Their work shows how much careful measurement can reveal, and how much was unknown before.

## Why it’s debated

Rigorous evaluation isn’t free, and not every program can or should run a randomized trial. Angus Deaton and Nancy Cartwright argue that trial results don’t carry over between places and populations as easily as people assume. Charitology takes both sides seriously: measure what matters, with methods proportionate to the decision being made. The sources here do not establish how large the measurement gap is across the sector.

## No one is to blame

A small team may need partners or shared services to evaluate its work. Limited evaluation capacity does not establish negligence.
