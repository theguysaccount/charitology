## What it looks like

A charity reports 10,000 meals served, 500 students tutored and 2,000 nets handed out. Those are *outputs*. Whether anyone is healthier, better educated or less hungry a year later is a different and harder question. Those are *outcomes*.

## Why it happens

Counting activity is cheap and can be done in-house. Measuring change usually takes comparison groups, follow-up surveys and statisticians, and few funders pay for that. The 2019 Nobel Prize in economics went to Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo and Michael Kremer for an experimental approach to measuring what anti-poverty programs really change. Their work shows how much careful measurement can reveal, and how much was unknown before.

## Why it’s debated

Rigorous evaluation isn’t free, and not every program can or should run a randomized trial. Angus Deaton and Nancy Cartwright argue that trial results don’t carry over between places and populations as easily as people assume. Charitology takes both sides seriously: measure what matters, with methods proportionate to the decision being made. How big the measurement gap really is across the sector has itself not been measured well.

## No one is to blame

A charity with three staff can’t run a study. The gap is a missing shared service, not negligence.
