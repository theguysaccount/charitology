## What it is

Guru Nanak (1469–1539), the first Guru and founder of the Sikh tradition, taught that the person who truly knows the way lives on what they earn by honest work and shares part of it with others. The line appears on page 1245 of the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, and it is the root of two practices.

*Dasvandh*, literally “a tenth part,” is the practice of giving one-tenth of one’s earnings, in the name of the Guru, to the common resources of the community. The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism calls it a religious obligation. Guru Amar Das, the third Guru, set up 22 districts, each led by a Sikh who taught and passed the community’s offerings to the Guru. When work on the sacred pool at Amritsar and the Harimandar shrine at its center began under Guru Ram Das, Sikhs were asked to set aside at least a tenth of their income for the common pool, collected by appointed ministers. Manuals of Sikh conduct from around the time of Guru Gobind Singh codified it.

The encyclopedia distinguishes dasvandh from ordinary charity. It meets the needs of the community: the gurdwaras, the Guru’s community kitchen and projects of social welfare and uplift.

## Seva

*Seva* means service, and in Sikh teaching it is also worship. Because God pervades creation, serving people is a way of serving God. The tradition recognizes service with the body, with the mind and with material means. Service with the body is considered the highest and is asked of every Sikh. In a society where manual labor was looked down on and assigned to the lowest castes, the Gurus made it an honored religious practice. They built it into institutions such as the *langar*, the Guru’s community kitchen, and into everyday service to the congregation, such as grinding grain and drawing water. In the langar, Britannica notes, everyone sits in one line, and the institution began as a protest against caste.

Giving through material means was deliberately made impersonal. Offerings and dasvandh went into the community’s common funds rather than from one person to another. The encyclopedia explains why: personal philanthropy can humiliate the person who receives it and swell the ego of the person who gives, while self-effacing service to the community is ennobling. Sikhs are particularly asked to serve the poor.

## Through the source lens

Seva’s purpose is devotion and the dissolving of the ego, not efficiency, yet the design is clear. Pooling gifts into shared institutions, and protecting the dignity of the person helped, echoes the higher rungs of [Maimonides’ ladder](/record/traditions/tzedakah/). A kitchen that feeds whoever comes is relief, and the common fund that keeps it running is structure.
