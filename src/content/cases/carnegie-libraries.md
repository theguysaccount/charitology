## What happened

Andrew Carnegie, the steel magnate, made his first library gift in 1881, to Dunfermline in Scotland, where he was born. By the time the last grants for library buildings were made in 1917, he and the Carnegie Corporation of New York had given some $56 million for 2,509 public libraries. According to the corporation, 1,681 of those buildings went up in the United States, in 1,412 communities.

The grants came with conditions. A town had to show that it needed a library, provide the site, keep the library free to all and promise yearly support equal to 10 percent of the grant, paid for by local taxes. Carnegie wrote that a free library was the best gift, “provided the community will accept and maintain it as a public institution.” His secretary, James Bertram, ran the program through one standard application form, the “Schedule of Questions,” and steered towns toward model floor plans. Money was released in stages as the building went up.

The libraries lasted. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas reports that more than half were still operating in the 21st century. In a working paper, the researchers Enrico Berkes and Peter Nencka compared towns that got a Carnegie library with towns that were approved for one but never built it, and found that patenting rose in the library towns over the next 20 years.

The program also followed local custom. Matthew Griffis, who directs a research project on Carnegie’s segregated libraries, writes that in most Southern communities, white attitudes dismissed the need for any library service for Black residents. He counts only eleven communities in the whole country that opened libraries for African Americans through Carnegie’s program.

## Where it sits in the system

The buildings were bricks. The lasting change was a rule: towns taxed themselves to run a free public library. That is **structure** (level 3) on the [depth scale](/method/#depth). It is also a counterexample to the [Short Horizon](/diagnosis/short-horizon/). Carnegie paid the one-time cost, and the gift was built to outlive him because the community took over the running costs.

The design gave towns a voice in a narrow sense. A library came only if local leaders asked for it and agreed to pay for it. But the people who decided were the people who held local power. Where Black residents were shut out of public life, the program worked within segregation, and the few libraries it funded for African Americans were separate ones. This is the [Missing Voice](/diagnosis/missing-voice/) in a different form: a gift that answers to “the community” serves whoever the community’s gatekeepers count.

## What carries over

Pair capital with a commitment to running costs, so the gift doesn’t die when the giver stops paying. Keep the terms simple enough to run at scale. And before relying on local consent, ask whose consent it is. Carnegie’s own philosophy of giving is in [The Gospel of Wealth](/record/traditions/the-gospel-of-wealth/).
