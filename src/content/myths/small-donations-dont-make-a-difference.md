## Why people believe it

The gifts that make the news are the giant ones: the nine-figure pledge, the name on the building. Next to a problem as large as hunger or disease, $20 can feel like nothing. And a small donor rarely hears what their particular gift did.

## What’s true

One small gift rarely changes an organization or a problem by itself, and its effect is usually invisible to the giver. Where a gift goes also matters more than its size. Comparing estimates for health programs, the philosopher Toby Ord found that moving money from the median program to the most cost-effective one would produce about 60 times the benefit. A small gift in the right place can outdo a much larger one in the wrong place.

## What’s false

That small gifts don’t matter. Added together they are a large part of the system. Giving USA estimates that charitable giving in the United States reached $617.2 billion in 2025, and individuals gave $394.2 billion of it, far more than foundations, bequests or corporations. That figure includes wealthy donors as well as small ones. After the 2010 Haiti earthquake, donors sent an estimated $43 million in small gifts by text message alone.

A small gift can also do real work on its own. The World Health Organization says a treatment with oral rehydration salts, which counter the dehydration that is diarrhea’s most severe threat, costs a few cents. GiveWell estimates that its top charities have typically saved a life for $3,000 to $5,500. At that rate, a $100 gift covers roughly 2 to 3 percent of the cost of saving a life, and GiveWell expects these costs to rise over time.

## What to ask instead

Not “is my gift big enough?” but “where will it do the most?” See [Most charities are about equally effective](/record/myths/most-charities-are-about-equally-effective/) and, on why big gifts get the glory, [the Prestige Economy](/diagnosis/prestige-economy/).
