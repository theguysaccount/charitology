U.S. charities that file the full IRS Form 990 report their expenses in Part IX, the Statement of Functional Expenses, which sorts them into three columns: program services, management and general, and fundraising. The **overhead ratio** is the share that falls in the second and third columns. A charity that spends $100 with $15 on management and fundraising has an overhead ratio of 15 percent.

## Why it became the default

It is printed on a public form, and it seems to answer the question donors most want answered: how much of my gift reaches the cause? No comparable number for results is printed anywhere.

## What it can’t tell you

The ratio says how costs are split, not what they buy. The costs of running an organization well, such as finance, data systems and management, land in the overhead columns, and they are often what make a program work. A charity can spend almost nothing on overhead and achieve nothing, and another can invest heavily in systems and change thousands of lives.

The ratio also shapes behavior. Ann Goggins Gregory and Don Howard described a *nonprofit starvation cycle*: funders expect low overhead, charities feel pressure to meet the expectation, so they underspend on infrastructure and underreport what they do spend, which keeps expectations low. They cited a study in which more than a third of organizations reported no fundraising costs at all, and a 2001 survey by the Better Business Bureau in which more than half of American adults said overhead should be 20 percent or less.

## An open letter

In June 2013 the leaders of GuideStar, Charity Navigator and the BBB Wise Giving Alliance, three organizations that donors turn to for information about charities, published an open letter calling the overhead percentage a poor measure of a charity’s performance. They asked donors to look instead at transparency, governance, leadership and results. They also said that very high overhead can still be a sign of fraud or poor management, which is the ratio’s legitimate use.

## What could replace it

Nothing equally simple has. That is why the habit persists, and why finding a public measure that tracks results without being easy to game is [Open Problem 6, the Overhead Replacement](/open-problems/the-overhead-replacement/). The raw material exists: [Charity Impact Media](https://theguysaccount.github.io/charity-impact-media/) publishes Form 990 spending breakdowns for more than 25,000 U.S. charities, with every number linked to its filing and no ratings attached. See also the [Myth-Busting Series](/record/myths/) on the claim that a low overhead ratio means a good charity.
