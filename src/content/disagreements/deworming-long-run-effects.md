## The debate

In 2004 Miguel and Kremer reported that a school deworming program in Busia, Kenya, cut absenteeism in treated schools by about a quarter and helped untreated children nearby, though it did not raise test scores. Later follow-ups estimated about 14 percent higher consumption, pooling the 15- and 20-year surveys, and 13 percent higher hourly earnings, pooling the 10-, 15- and 20-year surveys, among people assigned two to three additional years of treatment. Both estimates meet the 10 percent significance threshold, but not the conventional 5 percent threshold.

Systematic reviews read the wider evidence differently. A 2019 Cochrane review of 51 trials concluded that mass deworming does not appear to improve height, hemoglobin, cognition, school performance or mortality, and it cautioned against basing programs on two older studies that found large weight gains. A 2017 review found little to no effect on weight, height, cognition, attendance or mortality, and judged the long-run evidence too weak to draw conclusions.

In 2015 independent researchers published two reanalyses of the Kenya data, setting off what David Roodman, writing on GiveWell’s blog, later called the “great worm wars.” After coding errors were corrected, they found little evidence that attendance rose in neighboring schools, and evidence of better attendance in treated schools that depended on the method used and carried a high risk of bias. The original authors replied that the corrected data strongly support their findings on spillovers.

GiveWell took the third position. In 2016 it wrote that most of its staff thought deworming more likely than not to have very little or no impact, but at $0.50 to $1 per person treated, it applied a 1 to 2 percent multiplier to the trial results and still found the expected value worth funding. Roodman judged the earnings result credible but was less sure it would hold in other times and places.

## Where Charitology stands

Charitology grades deworming’s long-run evidence **mixed**: rigorous studies disagree, and the strongest positive evidence comes from one program. How much to stake on an uncertain chance of a large benefit is a value question, and Charitology doesn’t settle it. The evidence gap is clearer: long-run results from other times and places are what would move the debate.
