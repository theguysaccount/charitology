## The debate

GiveWell’s criteria state the case for proven programs. It looks for programs that have been studied rigorously and ideally repeatedly, estimates what they achieve per dollar and, since July 2022, recommends only charities it estimates to be at least ten times as cost-effective as cash transfers.

In a 2015 essay in the London Review of Books, Amia Srinivasan made the case from the other side. She argued that effective altruism works within existing institutions instead of questioning them, leaves structural causes of suffering such as trade, debt and labor exploitation unexamined, and judges political change with an expected-value arithmetic that can’t price radical change.

The third position points to evidence. In the United States, children’s average blood lead levels fell about 95 percent between the late 1970s and the 2010s, declines the researchers credit largely to federal regulation. That evidence comes from national health surveys, not a randomized trial, and J-PAL notes that randomization is hard when a policy applies to a whole country. An expert review convened by the International Agency for Research on Cancer found sufficient evidence that higher tobacco taxes cut smoking.

Attribution is the hard part. A World Bank account of the Philippines’ 2012 tobacco and alcohol tax reform credits a coalition of officials, legislators, development partners, medical professionals and civil society activists, and concludes that no single actor could have done it alone.

The fourth position points to programs that moved into government. Teaching at the Right Level was tested by researchers and a nonprofit, then run through government schools in Indian states. The first attempts failed and later versions worked; see the [entry](/record/interventions/teaching-at-the-right-level/).

## Where Charitology stands

Charitology’s [depth scale](/method/#depth) speaks directly to this question. It describes where work acts without grading it, so givers can see the mix they fund. The evidence shows that structural change can produce very large, measurable gains, and that credit for any one change is hard to assign. Whether to accept more uncertainty for a chance at deeper change is a value question Charitology doesn’t settle. It treats the measurement gap as the problem it can work on. See [measuring the source](/open-problems/measuring-the-source/), [the Symptom Trap](/diagnosis/symptom-trap/), [systemic change](/record/encyclopedia/systemic-change/), and the entries on [lead](/record/interventions/lead-exposure-elimination/) and [tobacco taxes](/record/interventions/tobacco-taxation-advocacy/).
