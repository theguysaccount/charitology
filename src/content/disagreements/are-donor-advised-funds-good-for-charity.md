## The debate

A donor-advised fund is an account held by a sponsoring charity. The IRS says the sponsor has legal control of the money once it is given, while the donor keeps advisory privileges over grants and investments.

The numbers are large. The DAF Research Collaborative’s annual report found that in 2024 donors contributed $89.6 billion to donor-advised funds, the funds granted $64.9 billion to charities, and assets reached $326 billion, up 27.5 percent from about $256 billion a year earlier. The aggregate payout rate was 25.3 percent.

Supporters point to the volume of grants and the aggregate payout rate. Comparisons with foundation distributions need care: the legal foundation minimum and the report’s DAF payout rate are different measures. The DAF rate divides grants by prior-year assets; it does not describe every account.

Critics see [parked money](/diagnosis/parked-money/). They argue that the funds give donors an immediate tax break while the rules on when the money must reach charity are loose, so the tax advantage may come without much public benefit.

The data cut both ways. Aggregate figures include outliers. The report distinguishes workplace and payment-processor accounts from other accounts; counting all of them together can hide important differences. And aggregate tax data can’t show why donors use the funds or what they would have given otherwise.

## Where Charitology stands

The money in these funds is already under the legal control of charitable sponsors, so the dispute is about timing and about what would have happened otherwise. How fast charitable money should move is a value question, and Charitology doesn’t settle it. How fast it actually moves is an empirical question that public data only partly answer, because the report is built from tax returns that give aggregate figures, not account-by-account behavior. That gap is [the payout question](/open-problems/the-payout-question/).
